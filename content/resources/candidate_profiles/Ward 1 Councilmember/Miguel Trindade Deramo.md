# Issues
## Affordability
### We deserve to live in a DC where we can all afford to stay in our homes, feed our families, and be able to enjoy all that the city has to offer. Housing availability and affordability are at the core of Miguel’s vision for a **Ward 1 that is affordable for everyone**. More people deserve to live here, and to do so affordably.

Housing is a human right, but we cannot guarantee access to housing that does not exist; that is why we need to significantly increase the supply of housing by making it easier, faster, and cheaper to build all types of housing, including both subsidized affordable housing and market-rate housing.

As a Councilmember, Miguel will support proven tools to build more housing while protecting tenants’ rights — that’s not an either-or. Tackling housing affordability and supply challenges requires a full-spectrum approach. We certainly need to build more homes in Ward 1 in order to bring down costs and increase access to housing; simultaneously, we can and must preserve affordability and protect tenants’ rights so that our neighbors can afford to continue living where they choose.

### Yes, More Housing in Ward 1
The District, including Ward 1, needs more homes. Cities like Minneapolis and Austin have used a combination of zoning reforms and streamlined permitting and approval processes to increase their supply of housing, which resulted in drops in rent or slower rent growth.

Step one is to work on easing regulatory barriers to housing production, from reforming on-site parking and setback requirements to expanding unnecessarily restrictive caps on building height. We are not limited to increasing density only on vacant land: we can and should also build housing on underutilized sites and over existing low-rise retail, especially along commercial corridors. **We also need to make changes to the Comprehensive Plan to facilitate additional density**, such as “missing middle” or “gentle density” housing, to create more for-sale and rental housing opportunities that are attainable for middle-income residents, young families, and first-time homebuyers.

As a Councilmember, Miguel will champion reforms to regulatory barriers to housing production, for example shortening permitting and approval processes to create housing more quickly and to lower unnecessary costs related to procedural delays. We can, and must, offer incentives, such as density bonuses, to encourage developers of market-rate housing to include affordable units in new multifamily properties. Miguel will make clear to the Mayor’s office his expectation that nominees to the Zoning Commission should similarly prioritize zoning changes that spur housing construction. Additionally, Miguel will conduct energetic oversight of our housing agencies as well as the Office of the Deputy Mayor for Planning & Economic Development, which is responsible for moving a number of Ward 1’s key redevelopment projects through the pipeline.

### Increasing Supply Isn't Enough
We need to build more affordable housing to ensure that our neighbors are not priced out of Ward 1 while we create new affordable housing to welcome new neighbors. As a Councilmember, Miguel will fight for more resources for the Housing Production Trust Fund (HPTF), while ensuring through rigorous oversight that our investment is effectively used, and paired with other resources, to create new affordable homes for households earning up to 50% of the Area Median Income.

Building new homes without preserving the existing affordable housing stock and implementing targeted solutions will lead to a net loss of affordable housing options and the displacement of neighbors with lower incomes. As a Councilmember, Miguel will support proven solutions to preserve affordability:

- Pairing the HPTF with a new Housing Preservation Fund with a mission to protect existing affordable housing, both subsidized and unsubsidized, so that it is not acquired (e.g., when affordability requirements expire) and turned into more expensive market-rate housing that lower-income families cannot afford.
    
- Reversing some of the negative revisions to the Tenant Opportunity to Purchase (TOPA) passed as part of the RENTAL Act. Miguel will work to reverse the last-minute amendment exempting two- to four-unit properties from TOPA, which denies thousands of renters the opportunity to potentially become homeowners by purchasing their homes or ensuring that their properties continue to serve as unsubsidized affordable housing. Miguel will also support shortening the 15-year TOPA exemption for new buildings.
    
- Making more frequent use of the District Opportunity to Purchase Act (DOPA). As a Councilmember, Miguel will work to create a financing tool for DOPA acquisitions; through oversight, he will ensure that the Department of Housing and Community Development is structurally healthy and capable of taking on more properties through DOPA. The District should also explore partnerships with values-aligned affordable-housing organizations to acquire and/or manage DOPA properties.
    
- Providing redevelopment incentives that require a one-to-one, or greater, replacement of affordable units for owners of affordable housing properties with significant maintenance backlogs. Miguel will champion the redevelopment of older public housing to significantly increase density and quality with “right to return” protections for residents.
    
- Exploring reforms to rent stabilization under the Rental Housing Act of 1985, including expanding the stock of existing multifamily properties that are subject to that law, while ensuring that rent caps do not hurt the financial feasibility of operating those properties amid rising costs like insurance, utilities, and maintenance. Such reforms could include a rolling, rather than fixed, timeframe for the applicability of rent stabilization.
    
### We Haven't Tried It All
The DC Council can’t keep implementing the same solutions and expect different outcomes. That’s why Miguel will support:

- **Maximizing the potential of housing developments on public land**. As a Commissioner, Miguel supported creating affordable housing at the 1617 U Street site. He is also well aware that when we draw out the development of affordable housing on public property over years of procedural hurdles, we not only prevent families from accessing affordable homes that are needed urgently; we also significantly increase the cost of building housing on these sites. Miguel will advocate to grant approval to proposed affordable housing developments on public property as a matter of right if they comply with zoning regulations.
    
- Taking a more active role in financing and developing various forms of social housing, which may include permanently affordable, mixed-income, publicly, and/or nonprofit-owned housing. Montgomery County’s approach serves as a potential model, and Miguel would support the creation of a fund to test such investments, adapted to the context of DC, with a growth plan toward a dedicated office or independent agency.
    
- Using oversight to push for better enforcement of housing standards and working with the Attorney General to identify and prosecute bad landlords.
    
### Bringing Down the Cost of Living in Other Ways
Miguel takes a broad view of affordability: housing isn’t the only factor. Too many jobs in the “new economy” come without benefits — that’s an area where labor and policymakers can ally to craft stronger worker protections. Basic needs, such as food and childcare, are also too expensive.

Miguel knows that **the vibrant neighborhoods he envisions must work for their own residents**. He will work to align incentives to preserve and grow the “missing middle,” the kinds of businesses that weave neighborhoods together: locally owned restaurants, corner stores, and services. They are the most critical for livability and yet are always at greatest risk of being priced out.

We should be extremely proud to be one of the leading cities in the country in **making childcare affordable for all families** and ensuring that those taking care of our children are compensated equitably. Miguel will ensure that our suite of early-childhood programs (universal pre-K, subsidies, the Pay Equity Fund, etc.) are on a sound financial footing, and will work to extend that support to fill the gap by providing childcare for ages 0 to 3. He also recognizes that protecting the District’s Earned Income Tax Credit and Child Tax Credit is also crucial: these are progressive and pioneering mechanisms for redistribution that support low- and moderate-income earners (even those without children in the home) in a very expensive city.
### Homelessness Is a Housing Issue
We can’t solve homelessness without addressing the root issue: a lack of affordable housing. Criminalization and the thoughtless clearing of encampments, such as we have seen in the past few years, are not the path to addressing homelessness in DC. Such actions only serve to disrupt supportive service delivery, stigmatize residents facing homelessness — who often lose their belongings during clearings — and pointlessly chase people from one jurisdiction to another.

As a Councilmember, Miguel will support a more effective, data-driven **“housing first” approach with no preconditions to accessing housing**. The process starts with robust hotline and outreach teams that connect neighbors experiencing homelessness to low-barrier shelters. Miguel will continue the District’s shift away from congregate shelters and toward non-congregate transitional housing with supportive services.

Miguel’s goal is to create more permanent supportive housing with resident services to address chronic homelessness, as well as help neighbors who have previously experienced homelessness to stay housed and to improve their health and financial conditions. Miguel’s vision for permanent supportive housing includes accommodation for different types of residents, including families, youth, and LGBTQ+ neighbors.

Operating permanent supportive housing does not always go smoothly. As a Commissioner, Miguel has worked with the management of a transitional facility in his district, the Terrell, to address complaints about inadequate HVAC, for example, which left families spending all day outside during the summer to avoid suffocating heat indoors. As a Councilmember, Miguel will scale up his ability to offer constituent services to residents experiencing homelessness, and in transitional programs, as well as oversight to ensure that residents are able to move through the lifecycle of services/Continuum of Care and “graduate” to stable situations, with special emphasis on systemic challenges in the crucial “lease-up” phase.
## Safe Streets

### You deserve to feel safe in DC, whether you’re biking to work in the morning or walking home late at night. As the Chairman of ANC 1B, which represents the U Street corridor, Miguel recognizes that safety means many things: safety from crime, safety from discrimination, safety while crossing the street. He knows that public safety is foundational and indispensable to healthy communities and thriving economies.

As a Councilmember, Miguel will support proven tools to combat violence in our neighborhoods and bolster upstream interventions to prevent violence in the first place. He will also work to reestablish trust between the community and MPD after the federal intervention — and refocus MPD on community-oriented policing.
### Public Safety is Built on Mutual Trust
Public trust in our police department (MPD) has seriously eroded since the federal intrusion on our autonomy. As a Councilmember, Miguel will be clear-eyed about the fact that **this loss of trust undermines everyone’s safety**.

Step one is to rededicate ourselves to transparency and accountability. That’s why Miguel led dozens of ANC Commissioners in calling on the DC Council to hold an oversight hearing on MPD’s policies and practices with respect to immigration enforcement. Miguel believes strongly that no operation involving MPD should include federal agents who conceal their identities or their agency affiliations, and no MPD personnel should take part in operations that lack clear legal authority and rigorous oversight.

However, the federal intervention is only the most recent source of tension between the community and MPD. We need to address deeper structural issues with policing in order to achieve lasting results:

- MPD is committed to “community policing,” but it has entirely abandoned its legal obligation to convene a community policing working group and publish a report on community policing every two years. **Community policing means working proactively in partnership** with the community to build strong relationships and collaboratively prevent crime. It means sending officers out to form real bonds of trust with neighbors, families, schools, and local businesses. We see far too little of this work in today’s MPD. Business owners on U Street have told Miguel that they’ve never met their local beat cops — but they have met National Guardsmen and federal agents. That is deeply wrong. As a Councilmember, Miguel will put pressure on the chief to restore the community policing working group and give it the tools to do a serious job. Miguel will work to get officers out of their cars and meaningfully interacting with the community, on the basis of mutual respect and trust-building.
    
- The conversation about police reform in DC is effectively dormant, despite the fact that the Police Reform Commission put a number of thoughtful proposals on the table more than four years ago, which deserve consideration. In particular, the commission advocated for a harm-reduction approach and, yes, more community policing. **Reform is all the more urgent now** that the federal surge has exposed so many of our neighbors — especially communities of color, our trans neighbors, and immigrant residents — to excessive, intrusive surveillance. As a Councilmember, Miguel will lead the charge to revive the most promising proposals for effective reform.
    
- MPD’s system of Citizens Advisory Councils (CAC) is meant to provide a conduit from the community directly into the police department, but they operate very unevenly and generally do not provide the kind of forum that other cities have achieved through independent oversight boards, for example. Miguel will work to evaluate the CACs and determine whether they can benefit from reform, whether a new mechanism is required, or both.
### Violence Interruption Saves Lives
Violence interruption (VI) is the District’s approach to preventing interpersonal conflict from turning into physical violence. It takes many forms, operating at the school, neighborhood, ward, and citywide levels.

As a Commissioner, Miguel sponsored two resolutions calling on the Council to maintain (or, better yet, increase) the District’s investment in VI programs from 2025 to 2026. Unfortunately, the Council chose to decrease overall funding for VI when it closed the Attorney General’s Cure the Streets program and concentrated all VI programming in the Office of Neighborhood Safety and Engagement (ONSE). The unified approach under ONSE is promising: one of its goals is to move from a 10-week to a 10-month commitment to at-risk clients, expanding from crime prevention to include work on wellness and employment. Successful implementation, though, will require consistent budgetary support and close Council oversight.

As a Councilmember, **Miguel will bolster VI programs**, while insisting that investments be based on rigorous research and data analysis. Miguel also believes in supporting VI at all levels, **especially the peacebuilding programs in schools**, which build on cognitive behavioral therapy and other approaches to teach young people to defuse conflict instead of reaching for weapons. Our Ward 1 schools should be learning from the success of peace programs at schools in other wards, and building interlinkages between school programs and “adult”/neighborhood-based VI programs. Miguel knows that DC has a tremendously dedicated and experienced VI community and he will work to provide adequate resources to scale up its efforts.

The federal intrusion in DC has exposed us — especially our immigrant, Black, and brown neighbors — to increased scrutiny, violence, and injustice. The Trump Administration has empowered ICE and other agencies to act with a Rambo attitude of impunity. They have caused real and permanent harm. None of this should have ever happened. As the child of an immigrant family and a former immigration professional, Miguel knows **how damaging, how counterproductive, and how un-American all of this is**.

Miguel does not support MPD cooperation with ICE. While he recognizes the inherent imbalance of power between the District and the federal government, Miguel will navigate that imbalance creatively with the power we do have to advance the best interests of DC residents. That’s what he has done as a Commissioner, and it’s what he’ll do as a Councilmember through a combination of oversight and advocacy.

DC has very clear laws on when it is appropriate for individuals with violent criminal records to be turned over to federal authorities after they present lawful documentation, including a judicial warrant, to take them into custody. Local law enforcement resources should be reserved to maintain DC’s public safety, not carry out federal immigration law enforcement, which is solely the responsibility of the federal government. As a Councilmember, Miguel will **introduce legislation to amend the Sanctuary Values Amendment Act of 2020 (SVAA)**, which governs interactions between the District government (including MPD) and federal immigration agencies. In particular, his bill will:

·        Expand the prohibition on information sharing to cover not only individuals in custody but also those detained, such as in traffic stops. This will ensure that routine interactions with MPD do not create a pretext for federal involvement, including by ICE and CBP;

·        Expand the reporting requirements for the MPD and increase the frequency of reporting data to the Mayor and Council. Currently, the SVAA requires MPD to provide a report annually to the Mayor and Council about the “number of requests for information or detainer made by a federal immigration agency [...] and any action taken by the District agency in response to such a request.” The Council has not required MPD to track and report on the number of times MPD shared information on persons not in its custody, provided transportation for federal agents or detainees, operated adjacent to a federal arrest, or witnessed a lethal use of force by federal personnel. Miguel’s bill will incorporate this type of data, as well as changing the reporting requirement from annual to quarterly or even monthly, so as to inform our decision-making amid a rapidly changing legal landscape;

·         Create protections against warrantless civil immigration enforcement at sensitive locations, including clinics, hospitals, schools, and places of worship, as many other jurisdictions have done. These protections will likely trigger challenges in court, which is why Miguel will work closely with the Office of the Attorney General to craft this language sharply. It is important to codify these protections so that engaging in essential interactions, like dropping off one’s children at school or seeking medical care, does not create a window of opportunity for ICE;

·        Prohibit the use of face coverings by law enforcement officers, especially immigration officers, much as California has done. Miguel’s bill will also mandate that, in most cases, law enforcement officers must wear agency-identifying insignia as an essential measure of transparency and accountability.

Let’s remember why we have sanctuary policies in the first place. First, they promote community safety by ensuring access to courthouses and police stations without fear of immigration enforcement. When residents, especially those in vulnerable situations, feel unable to access justice, more crimes go unreported — a “chilling effect” that undermines everyone’s safety. Likewise, schools and hospitals must remain neutral zones to guarantee that children can enjoy their right to education and patients can reliably access necessary care.

Second, we have sanctuary policies because they preserve our local resources for local priorities, rather than doing ICE’s job for them. Immigration law, which is federal, is the purview of the federal government to enforce; the federal government should not direct local resources, which are funded by the local taxes we pay. Sanctuary policies thus protect everybody from federal overreach and are an essential means of preserving what little autonomy DC has.

The real solution to our vulnerability, of course, is **statehood and the full representation in Congress** that we deserve as Americans. That will always remain our North Star and our most fervent demand. It is a civil-rights and a human-rights imperative, period.

#### **Taking on Rats**

Rats aren't just a nuisance — they are dangerous to public health and pets. At the same time, Miguel understands we cannot poison our way out of this human-animal conflict. The overuse of rodenticides is having negative impacts on our wildlife. Animal rescue nonprofits often encounter deceased or distressed wildlife animals who are victims of rodenticide poisoning. That is why his plan to better manage rats in DC revolves around four main planks that do not include rodenticides:

First, we need to replace our open-air trash cans with rodent-proof closed bins. District’s open-air rat buffets, formally known as litter bins — the black metal ones you see on many street corners — must be replaced by a rodent-proof model for effective waste management. DC should also explore innovative waste management solutions used in other jurisdictions, such as underground trash containers that are largely hidden with only small deposit bins visible above, to improve sanitation, reduce odors, and enhance aesthetics. 

Second, DC should expand its composting pilot into a permanent program. We should be encouraging as many people as possible to participate, especially people in apartments who, at this moment, are often left out of the composting program. We can reduce the rodent problem upstream by cutting down on the amount of food waste in the trash. Ward 1 currently has only four smart bins, all conspicuously located in the eastern part of the ward; I’m proud to say that we’re also using them more than any other ward, as measured by tonnage since the beginning of the program. 

Third, better enforcement. As the Chairman of ANC 1B, which represents U Street, Miguel has seen many alleys full of carelessly dumped trash that is proving to be a feast for rats. We need to empower the Alcoholic Beverage and Cannabis Administration (ABCA) and the Department of Public Works (DPW) to enforce the laws that are already on the books to remove illegal trash dumping and clean our alleys.

Miguel believes the same enforcement we conduct for businesses should also apply to landlords — not only with illegal trash dumping, but also with abatement. Large landlords are currently getting away with abatements that are far too lenient to fully cover all of their units. We should require that abatement contracts meaningfully cover the full property, not half-measures. As a Councilmember, Miguel will also use oversight to ensure the proper implementation of the Containing Litter and Ensuring Adequate Neighborhood (CLEAN) Collections Amendment Act, which requires adequate trash and recycling cans and more frequent waste collection schedules in DC’s multifamily buildings.

Lastly, Miguel wants to innovate. DC government can’t keep implementing the same solutions and expecting different outcomes. We need to prioritize effective waste-management practices while also being creative with new solutions. DC can launch field trials of rodent birth control, a tool that has been implemented in other cities, such as Boston and Chicago. These are safe alternatives that can humanely reduce rodent populations.

As a Councilmember, Miguel will work on advancing provisions included in the Rodent Abatement and Transparency (RAT) Amendment Act and Rodent Abatement Through Smart Solutions (RATSS) Amendment Act that fit nicely within his rat plan, including:

- Requiring DC Health to submit periodic reports, covering progress on and resources needed for advancing rat management efforts;
    
- Improving interagency coordination by automatically referring rat activity found during DPW trash inspections to DC Health;
    
- Providing DC Health with the authority to charge property owners and managers for the cost of rodent treatment when they fail to resolve infestations;
    
- Replacing existing waste cans that are in disrepair with rodent-proof bins; 
    
- Reviving the commercial trash compactor grant program, which provided financial assistance to DC restaurants and food businesses to help them purchase trash compactors — machines that compress food waste into sealed containers.

#### CASE STUDY
##### Where did our streateries go?
Streateries became a part of DC’s landscape during the pandemic, when restrictions on indoor dining prompted the District to convert some public space (sidewalks, parking spaces) into places where people could gather in a way that was safe for the community as well as profitable for local businesses. Predictably, almost all of the streateries we’ve known and enjoyed — especially in Adams Morgan and Mount Pleasant — disappeared overnight. **This was a signal failure of government agencies to develop policy collaboratively, and of the DC Council to remedy an unworkable situation in advance.** Businesses feel like the DC government caught them in a bait-and-switch, and residents feel robbed of something fun that benefited the community. Miguel knows that many of the temporary streateries leftover from the pandemic were outdated and unsafe; that’s why his ANC passed a resolution calling for those older structures to be removed. But effectively killing streateries citywide was a mistake — the result of poor planning and a lack of coordination. Miguel is committed to designing a permanent streatery program that will beautify and activate our streetscapes while allowing businesses to thrive.

As your Councilmember, Miguel will continue to champion the bus and its riders, working to advance meaningful infrastructure and service improvements across Ward 1.

Ward 1 is home to indispensable public gathering spaces like the Park at LeDroit, the African American Civil War Memorial Plaza, Columbia Heights Civic Plaza, and Lamont Plaza. Our neighbors deserve well-maintained spaces to sit down outside — whether to rest, wait for the bus, or catch up with a friend. The District government has systematically removed public benches across the District, including in Ward 1, depriving neighbors of the ability to just sit down. This backwards trend has also applied to public trash and recycling cans across the city, meaning increased litter and food for rats. Miguel will work to reverse these trends, using smart “placemaking” design to offer neighbors more places to be outside and more ways to discard waste responsibly.
    
- **More trees and more shade**. Street trees provide shade and improve comfort in hot weather, making streets cooler, healthier, and more inviting for everyone who uses them. Ward 1 is one of the hottest parts of the District during the summer and most subject to the heat island effect — threatening the health and safety of all residents but especially seniors, those with chronic health conditions, and our unhoused neighbors. We deserve a bold plan to mitigate excess heat while making our streets more beautiful. Miguel will work for this investment including prioritizing investments in expanding the tree canopy in areas with extreme heat like Ward 1, cool pavement that reduces street temperatures, and reinstating incentives for green roofs.
## Education
### Miguel is the proud son of a schoolteacher. He learned so much from his mother — about the importance of showing up, about meeting people where they are, and about service that’s bigger than yourself. He saw her sacrifices of time and money to provide for her students, including buying extra supplies out of her own pocket and spending her weekends (with little Miguel in tow) taking students to academic tournaments. These experiences taught Miguel that proper compensation for teachers and robust support for teacher wellness are crucial to positive educational outcomes.
Miguel recognizes his own good fortune to have attended public schools with strong extracurricular and arts programs, and is determined to secure the same for DC’s children today. Thanks to his dedicated teachers, Miguel learned to play musical instruments, compose music, and produce plays; he loved these activities so much that, as a high schooler, he co-founded a summer theater troupe for younger children to experience the stage. (He wrote all of the original musical scores himself!) All of that was possible because of supportive teachers, school administrators, and parents.

DC’s families deserve schools that can match the dreams they have for their children. DC has one of the highest per-pupil investments in education in the country, and Miguel is proud to live in a city that prioritizes its youth. At the same time, he recognizes that more can be done to use those resources efficiently to actually support students and teachers in the classroom. As a Councilmember, Miguel is committed to pairing our investment with accountability measures to ensure that DC Public Schools (DCPS) and charter schools deliver high-quality education, that the DC Department of General Services (DGS) provides dignified spaces in which our students can learn, and that we uphold our commitment to making early childhood education accessible for all families.

Miguel has committed to conducting annual walkthroughs of every school in Ward 1, in advance of each new school year, to inspect facilities first-hand and to make himself accessible to educators and parents alike. Too many of our schools are not physically conducive to learning — and that’s squarely within the Council’s power to fix.
### Students Need to Attend School to Learn
The most pressing issue we face, and the most fundamental, is that **too many of our children cannot read at grade level and cannot do basic math**. This is a particular issue for our ward because Ward 1 schools have posted some of the city’s lowest test scores. Moreover, ours was the only ward in the city to make virtually no improvement in test scores last year. Many factors are at play, but a key one is attendance. Students need to attend school to learn.

More than one-third (37%) of students in Ward 1 schools are “chronically absent” — meaning that they have missed more than 10% of the school year. In fact, Ward 1 recently posted the highest year-over-year increase in chronic absenteeism in the District. We must address the specific drivers of absenteeism given the unique profile of students in Ward 1. Miguel understands that that will require a comprehensive plan that provides students, families, educators, and mission-driven organizations with the support they need to ensure regular attendance.

First, we need tailored, culturally sensitive outreach efforts in our ward, which has the highest percentage of English learners in DC. As a Latino who grew up in a multigenerational immigrant family, Miguel understands the toll that recent changes in immigration enforcement have taken on our immigrant families, which has also impacted the attendance of students from immigrant households. We need to ensure that these families are connected to language-appropriate communications with information on resources and support services. As a Councilmember, Miguel will introduce legislation to amend the Sanctuary Values Amendment Act to provide protections against warrantless civil immigration enforcement at sensitive locations like schools.

Second, we should expand the DC’s Truancy Reduction Pilot to cover more Ward 1 schools, enabling school counselors to refer students experiencing chronic absenteeism to the DC Department of Human Services (DHS) to offer these students and their families supportive services without court involvement. The pilot currently only covers Cardozo High School in Ward 1. As a Councilmember, Miguel will also support the Improving School Attendance Outcomes Amendment Act, which would permanently transfer referrals of students from the District of Columbia's child welfare agency (CFSA) to DHS, which is better equipped to direct families to language-accessible support services.

Third, we should improve the effectiveness and expand the geographic outreach of DC’s Safe Passage program in Ward 1, to include more neighborhoods like Mount Pleasant and Park View. The Safe Passage Safe Blocks should be better utilized to help Ward 1 students commute safely to their schools through high-traffic corridors like Columbia Road, 14th Street, and Georgia Avenue. As a Councilmember, Miguel will also champion improving the bus service in Ward 1 to ensure that students can commute to their schools through a reliable and frequent bus service.
### Our Students Must Feel Safe Everywhere
DC’s students deserve to feel safe all the time: before, during, and after school hours. The best public-safety strategy is to prevent violence and crime before they occur.

As chairman of the ANC representing U Street, Miguel viscerally understands the impact of huge youth gatherings on local residents and businesses. Fundamentally, Miguel is not in favor of youth curfews; he does not support a permanent curfew or repeatedly extending the curfew as the only solutions. Miguel also wants to prevent unnecessary interactions between our youth and policing or the criminal justice system.

Our leaders have told us repeatedly that the curfew is only a band-aid, but they have not advanced solutions to the underlying issues. The Mayor has a Youth Advisory Council (YAC) that could serve as a proactive conduit for offering advice to decision-makers before problems arise; unfortunately, the YAC has been essentially defunct for years. As a Councilmember, Miguel will shape law and oversight to pressure the Mayor to reactivate the Youth Advisory Council and advance programming co-led by youth, including with schools, recreation centers, DC agencies, and entertainment businesses.

He will also look to broaden the city’s partnerships with businesses to program all-ages events like night markets, provide safe spaces at city facilities like recreation centers, and encourage youth-led programming. Nightlife is not the only type of activity that appeals to young people. Our communities can do much more to foster third spaces that are welcoming of youth.

At the Council, Miguel will work to direct MPD’s enforcement toward the adults who plan and promote unlicensed gatherings, as well as those who sell alcohol illegally at such events. He will also support stabilizing funding for Violence Interruption at all levels, including schools. Specifically, Miguel will support strengthening and expanding DC’s Pathways 2 Peace leadership program, which offers support to eligible young adults who are most at risk of being involved in gun violence, including CBT-informed training, peace circles, wellness activities, and volunteer opportunities.

Miguel will also support innovative programs such as the DCPS partnership with Our Minds Matter, an organization dedicated to equipping teens with mental health education, resources, and support. Through this partnership, all DCPS schools have a Youth Mental Health Ambassadors club, which enables student ambassadors to host events and create campaigns centered on mental health topics.

To strengthen the safety and wellbeing of our students, Miguel supports improving and expanding DC's Out-of-School Time (OST) system. This includes working with community providers to expand OST seat capacity to meet the needs of more DCPS students, increasing the capacity of staffing that requires specialized training, and increasing transportation accessibility for students who want to participate in OST.

Miguel also supports improving and strengthening programs that offer our youth meaningful employment opportunities that can help them build skills, such as the Mayor Marion S. Barry Summer Youth Employment Program and the longer-term High School Internship Program. He also supports a legislative proposal that would offer eligible youth a paid year of employment at jobs that support the DC government priorities.
### Transparency and Accountability in Education
The Council’s oversight of education in recent years has largely focused on transportation for special-education students and the affordability of early childhood education. These are crucial pillars of DC’s education system and clearly warrant oversight. However, the core question to determine the success of any education system is: **Are students learning?**  
  
While the city-level academic growth we see is heartening, test scores (to use but one metric) are persistently low across the District. As of last school year, only one out of three students was proficient in English Language Arts (ELA), and only one out of four students was proficient in math. This is especially a concern for Ward 1, with our schools posting some of the city’s lowest test scores; alarmingly, we were the only ward to make virtually no improvement in test scores from the 2023–24 to the 2024–25 school year.  
  
Miguel has repeatedly called for the next term of the DC Council to be “an education Council” that takes seriously its moral responsibility to prepare students for adult life. The Council should be more involved in and more thoughtful about accountability for performance at DCPS and charter schools alike, and ensuring that our practices are evidence-based, especially in core subjects such as literacy and math. As a Councilmember, Miguel will hold DCPS and charter schools accountable to the same standards and levels of accountability. Miguel will push for making the Public Charter School Board meetings, in which decisions about charter schools are made, more transparent and public. Miguel will also champion holding ward-level school performance listening sessions and meetings to identify barriers to success and identify solutions to fill any gaps in collaboration with parents and educators.

Oversight efforts should be focused on achievable outcomes, rather than on how schools have spent allocated resources. However, Miguel will hold DCPS accountable for how additional funding allocated to schools serving students designated as at-risk (those for receiving food or income assistance) is spent and reported. Recent analyses show that some schools spend at-risk funds on core operations rather than specific programs targeting at-risk students.
### Every School Should be Well-Resourced
Students need a safe, healthy, and clean physical environment to learn and thrive. At the Council, Miguel will champion ensuring DCPS schools in Ward 1 and the district are well-resourced and maintained. It is unacceptable that the DC Department of General Services (DGS) leaves maintenance requests for our public schools unaddressed for months. As a Councilmember, Miguel will hold DGS accountable for not promptly addressing work orders in DCPS schools, which include roof leaks, sewage leaks, and HVAC failures. This includes fixing the HVAC systems at Ward 1’s Cardozo Education Campus and Tubman Elementary School, where far too many classrooms are either too hot or cold throughout the year, and addressing the elevator issue at the Columbia Heights Education Campus (CHEC).

As a Councilmember, Miguel will use legislative and oversight authority to set minimum standards for DCPS physical quality and mechanisms for promptly addressing maintenance issues. He will also personally conduct school walk-throughs before each school year begins to make sure that maintenance issues are documented and addressed, like former Councilmember Cheh did. That is about showing up for our students, but also for our teachers, so they have a safe and conducive workplace.

Miguel also acknowledges that students need non-educational resources to support their wellbeing at school. As a Councilmember, Miguel will be a leader in ensuring that schools are sufficiently staffed with nurses, counselors, and social workers. Specifically, he will champion fully funding and expanding the School-Based Behavioral Health program, which integrates licensed clinicians from community-based organizations (CBOs) into schools to provide early interventions intended to avoid crises.

At the Council, Miguel will ensure that any transition of behavioral health care from CBOs to the DC Department of Behavioral Health (DBH) is meticulously planned so as to avoid disruption of services and, ultimately, to improve the availability and quality of care. Our students must not suffer because of bureaucratic bungling. The current transition proposal has raised the alarm about DBH’s lack of requisite staff capacity to deliver services directly. This issue is critical for Ward 1 as many trusted, local CBOs like Mary's Center will be discontinued from providing mental health services to students. Additionally, Miguel supports shoring up and expanding the DCPS Connected Schools Model, which enables participating schools to function as resource hubs for students and their families, by offering access to resources related to employment, housing, legal aid, health, and more.
### Early Childhood Education is as Important as K-12 Education
Miguel understands that early childhood education is equally as important as K-12 education. Our early childcare centers are key to ensuring that our students show up ready on their first day of elementary school. Early childcare educators are not just offering childcare; they are preparing our youngest learners for success when they start school. 

We should be extremely proud to be one of the leading cities in the country trying to make childcare more affordable for all families and ensuring that those taking care of our children are compensated equitably. However, more can be done to extend support and fill in gaps in access. On average, DC families spend 26% of their income on childcare for one infant, which significantly exceeds the federal standard of affordability of 7% of family income. The recent closure of the voucher list for daycares has had complex ramifications; for example, many students at DC’s Opportunity Academies are themselves parents, and will be unable to attend school if they cannot access affordable care for their children.  
  
As a Councilmember, Miguel will ensure that our suite of early-childhood programs, including universal pre-K, the Childcare Subsidy Program, and the Pay Equity Fund, is on a sound financial footing to support families in accessing affordable childcare services. Miguel champions restoring and protecting funding for the Pay Equity Fund, to reverse the resulting subsidy waitlist that we are witnessing for the first time since the launch of the program, which was induced by the FY25 funding cuts.

Miguel will also work to fill the gap by providing financial support for affordable childcare for ages 0 to 3. While the Birth-to-Three for ALL DC Act calls for helping families pay no more than 10% of their income on childcare for children aged 0-3 with a focus on the lowest-income families, the law remains severely underfunded. Increasing funding for this measure would enable over 12,000 infants and toddlers to benefit from subsidized childcare services.  

As an urbanist, Miguel is interested in exploring ways to overcome zoning and code barriers to having childcare centers on floors that are above the ground floor in commercial buildings, while ensuring that these facilities have safe, rapid evacuation plans for fire hazards. DC can learn lessons from other cities like Seattle, which allow operating childcare centers on upper floors and in nearly all areas regardless of zoning. These changes may result in more-affordable childcare centers, including in vacant upper floors in some mixed-use buildings along main corridors.

Miguel recognizes that protecting the District’s Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) and Child Tax Credit (CTC) is also crucial: these are progressive and pioneering mechanisms for redistribution that support low- and moderate-income earners amid an affordability crisis in DC. Miguel supports the efforts championed by Councilmember Parker which resulted in strengthening funding for DC’s EITC and CTC (collectively expected to increase economic security for nearly 78,000 children and their families). Miguel has consistently called for reconstituting the Tax Revision Commission, which sought to rebalance and broaden the tax base in order to, among other goals, protect the durability of the CTC.
## Labor
### From a young age, Miguel understood the importance of laws and systems that protect workers and provide them with the financial and legal support they need to thrive.
Miguel was raised in a “mixed” family: his mother was a teacher in a non-unionized private school, whereas his father spent his entire career as a member of the Communications Workers of America (CWA). Miguel’s uncles were also electricians and members of the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers (IBEW). Miguel saw from an early age that his father and uncles had access to resources, protections, and advancement in a way that his mother did not — even though she had more educational and professional credentials. Miguel’s mother eventually chose to leave her profession due to those types of structural factors.

More recently, Miguel saw viscerally the value of unions when his partner, Luis, was terminated along with all of his colleagues from the U.S. Agency for International Development (USAID). This was an utter calamity in Luis’s and his peers’ lives. Amid the chaos of shuttering an entire government agency (with dubious legality), it was only the unions that had the capacity to fight for their members’ rights, both directly with the government and through the various lawsuits they filed to enforce the protections owed to public-sector employees.

Unions are increasingly relevant in our society once again precisely because workers are being disadvantaged in industries where corporate acquisitions and consolidations are trending toward monopolies/monopsonies and diminished competition in the labor market. It is appropriate for elected officials to collaborate with unions to set high labor standards that create upward pressure throughout the economy, and to exercise oversight functions robustly to ensure that labor agreements are respected and that workers, both union and otherwise, are justly compensated and protected.

As a Commissioner, Miguel has pushed the envelope of what ANCs can do by conducting public oversight in the name of worker protections. A structural collapse at a construction site occurred in his commission area in late 2024, which resulted in the death of a worker. As the Chairman of ANC 1B, Miguel has worked consistently since then to accompany investigations by the Department of Buildings (DOB) and the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA). He invited representatives of DOB to multiple meetings to discuss their preliminary findings, their published after-action report, and the ongoing policy changes resulting from the accident. The ANC oversight has resulted in substantive changes inside DOB and has raised the incident to the attention of the Council. Miguel is intent on honoring the memory of the victim, Edgar, and his family by seeking justice as well as reforms that will prevent similar tragedies in the future. This is not a typical activity for an ANC, but Miguel has encouraged and coordinated this important undertaking because he did not have faith that DOB’s internal process would yield sufficient accountability. This is the tenacity that Miguel will bring to the Council and its all-important oversight functions.
### Provide Better Worker Protections
DC should be proud of the Displaced Workers Protection Act, which provides protection for workers in specific industries in DC's contract economy. The Council has expanded the scope of the law at least twice since 1994, indicating that the Act has worked well and that the Council believes workers in other sectors can benefit from it.

The Council also holds the responsibility of ensuring that the Act is faithfully implemented. One notable problem with the Act is that the Department of Employment Services does not enforce the Act; only a lawsuit can provide remedy to a worker whose rights under the Act are violated. Lawsuits are complex and expensive, and most workers do not have the resources to pursue justice from the courts. Empowering DC’s Department of Employment Services (DOES) to enforce the Act would be one relatively easy fix for the Council to make. That would also enable the Council to conduct oversight of DOES’s enforcement of the Act, which would keep the Council better informed as to how the law is truly performing on the ground.

The Council can also work with the Attorney General to identify and prosecute bad-actor employers. As a Councilmember, Miguel will ensure that OAG has the staff and budget needed for this kind of enforcement.

While the Council also has the power to compel testimony from contractors through subpoenas, the Council does not use this authority frequently. As a councilmember, Miguel will conduct robust oversight, beyond the routine, once-annual performance oversight and budget hearings for government agencies. If laws are being violated, the Council can and must act to collect facts, refer matters for prosecution when appropriate, and update the laws to keep pace with a changing economy.
### Improve District Procurement Processes
DC’s public bodies should set high standards for working conditions and benefits. Everyone who contributes to the functioning of a workplace should be treated with equal dignity. The division between a direct-hire professional staff that enjoys certain protections, and a contracted service staff that does not, often serves to reinforce and perpetuate deeper divisions of class, race, gender, and immigration status. Miguel worked for years in the federal government, where increasing contracting and outsourcing have fractured the workforce into many disjointed segments. He understands how the patchwork of different hiring mechanisms, benefits, and protections creates inequalities, diminishes morale, and impedes career growth. Our District government should model different behavior and treat its workforce equitably.

As a Councilmember, Miguel will support reforms that address this issue. He will explore legislative changes that would require employers receiving DC contracts with a specific floor (e.g., $500,000 or more) to provide workers with essential benefits, including employee-sponsored health insurance coverage. At the Council, Miguel will use his oversight authority to ensure that DOES is tracking First Source Employment Act compliance. The Act requires employers on government-sponsored projects or contracts of $300,000 or more to hire DC residents for a specific share of all new jobs created through the project and to ensure that a certain share of apprenticeship hours are worked by DC residents. He will also explore reforms that would strengthen the “Good Faith” waiver standard to ensure that contractors have justifiable reasons for hiring non-DC residents and hold contractors liable for subcontractors’ First Source compliance.
### Ensure that Workers Receive Fair Wages
DC voters twice passed a ballot initiative to provide service industry workers with better wages and wage protections. Another ballot measure that would increase the minimum wage to $25, while eventually eliminating the tip credit, will be considered by DC voters soon. Ensuring fair and livable wages is essential to supporting DC workers amid an affordability crisis. In doing so, we must be thoughtful about increasing wages in a way that does not unintentionally disrupt our local economy and lead to negative impacts like job cuts — especially at a time of already-high unemployment and diminished consumer spending due to Donald Trump’s DOGE and his reckless federal intervention.

As a steering committee member and lead organizer for Initiative 83 (ranked-choice voting), Miguel is extremely sensitive to the importance of ballot initiatives and referenda as legitimate tools of direct democracy. He also initiated and led a District-wide effort two years ago to defeat a bill in Council, the Initiative Amendment Act, that would have dramatically gutted the people’s power to propose ballot measures. Miguel does not stand for attacks on democracy — especially not in DC, where we already lack so many fundamental rights, and especially not at this moment in which our self-government is under threat.

Miguel does not believe in overturning ballot measures as a general rule; he believes in focusing — in an early and sustained way — on getting the implementation right, which is the proper role of the Council.

Initiative 82 was a laudable attempt to raise standards and protect workers. However, the implementation of 82 has been so poor that many of the people it aimed to protect are feeling hurt, overlooked, and ultimately disadvantaged by its effects. That is a failure of policymakers to respond to the clear mandate from the electorate. The Council has been exclusively reactive to Initiative 82. As we look ahead to a minimum-wage initiative, Miguel will push his colleagues on the Council to proactively and deliberately craft policy to achieve a smooth transition. That should include early discussions with the proposers of the ballot measure to work out, in good faith, procedural and policy details in advance of the finalization of the initiative text.

At the Council, Miguel will push to use proven tools that have helped other jurisdictions achieve a smooth transition in wage increases. One example is Seattle, which implemented its Minimum Wage Ordinance by creating a tiered, gradual system to protect small local businesses from unintended economic shocks; devising a transition plan through negotiations with labor unions, business owners, and community members; tying wage increases to inflation rather than basing them on political discourse; and having third parties (such as universities and think tanks) track the actual on-the-ground outcomes. It goes without saying that DC did none of these things in attempting to implement Initiative 82.

The lesson here is that **progressivism depends on successful implementation.** Legitimacy is performance-based. Initiative 82 was implemented so poorly that not only do many DC residents regret it, throwing up their hands in frustration whenever they see a restaurant bill, but policymakers in other states point to it as a reason not to pursue higher wages in their own jurisdictions. That is a massive setback for progressivism and workers’ rights nationally. We must do better, especially in planning for and implementing any future wage increases.
