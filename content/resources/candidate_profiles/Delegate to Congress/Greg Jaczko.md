# Press Releases from Jaczko's Substack
## Naturally, Utility Bills are too Expensive

### A Policy Brief on DC’s Electricity Crisis
Utility bills are no place for public policy. Yet DC has turned yours into a vehicle for it. The result? Sky-high bills stacked with fees and surcharges — many of them quietly subsidizing benefits for higher-income households. Pile that on top of an already skyrocketing electricity market and you’ve got a crisis that’s going to get worse.

I’ve spent years on early stage development of offshore wind and solar projects and fighting the nuclear industry’s attempts to crowd out renewables. DC’s approach is self-defeating . It’s creating unsustainably high electricity bills that will ultimately generate a backlash against the very renewable energy efforts we need. I believe in this transition. That’s exactly why I’m saying DC is doing it wrong. **A sustainable system must use sensible, affordable approaches. DC is doing the opposite — slowing solar adoption and raising everyone’s electricity bills.** Fixing it will take time, but systemic reforms now can blunt the future increases locked into our system.
#### How DC’s Electricity System Actually Works.
Washington, DC is part of the PJM wholesale electricity market — it’s like Costco but for power. You buy in bulk, you don’t pick the supplier, and you don’t see an itemized receipt. When the electricity industry started, states carved out exclusive service territories for single companies — a regulated monopoly we call a utility. Over time, some states broke off the power generation side, creating competitive wholesale markets. The largest is PJM, which serves DC and most of the Mid-Atlantic.

**Pepco makes money off the assets it owns — the poles, wires, substations, and transmission lines throughout the city. Everything they spend, they charge to you.** And make money they do. Pepco’s parent company Exelon reported $799 million in profit last year across its utility holdings. Assuming DC’s share is about 20%, that’s roughly $160 million annually that DC ratepayers pay Pepco — just as profit. And no matter how expensive electricity gets, that profit is guaranteed [^1].
#### Fix #1: Take the Profit Out of It
Washington is the perfect place for a public power authority. Unlike Pepco — an investor-owned utility whose monopoly structure guarantees shareholders a profit — a public power authority is a government entity that makes no profit and pays no taxes. Remember: Pepco doesn’t pay taxes.** You do. Ratepayers cover taxes, generation costs, **and any other cost of doing business.**

Servicing the electricity system in DC is not complex. There are no generators, because Pepco can’t own them and DC has no space for them regardless. It just builds and maintains the system, gets power to your home or business, keeps track of the energy supplier and sends you a bill. **A public power authority can do that with instant savings on taxes and the profit that drives those taxes.**

DC hasn’t done this yet because utilities know they have a nearly flawless business. As long as they keep building and expanding their asset base, they’re guaranteed a return. The public utility commission grants them about 10%. They can never lose money.

Governments can buy out the utility assets, but Pepco will argue they deserve far more than a fair price. **Studies consistently show public power customers pay on average 13% less than investor-owned utility customers.**[^2] Here’s the reality: DC ratepayers already paid for all that infrastructure through their bills. The real price should reflect what’s still owed, not what a third-party buyer would pay. I will introduce legislation to require these transactions to complete within 18 months at residual value. That’s one unnecessary expenditure for DC residents of $160 million per year. On an average residential electricity bill of about $190, roughly $25 is paying for Pepco’s profit. That needs to change.

Of course, that number is hidden within all the rate charges and costs you see on your bill. They don’t list it. **At a minimum, DC residents should see how much of their bill pays for Pepco profits.**
#### Fix #2: Make Data Centers Pay Their Tab
DC must be part of a larger power market, because we simply don’t have space for our own power plants. We import about 95% of the electricity we need. Additionally, competitive power markets generally produce cheaper power than traditional integrated utility systems. Nonetheless in PJM, prices have spiked. The driver is the data center boom — a modern-day gold rush in Virginia, especially — and it’s pushing up the price of every kilowatt Pepco has to buy for the district. **This increase has led to skyrocketing prices for the electricity Pepco must buy to serve the district.**[^3]

Major data center expansions are already planned for the next several years. Data center demand drove an 833% spike in PJM capacity auction prices for 2025–2026.[^4] The tsunami of price increases will keep flooding the district.[^5]

Like transitioning to public power, solutions require federal legislation. Data centers must be responsible for the direct and indirect costs associated with their development. Legislation, while complex and challenging, is the only path to slow the increases.
#### Fix #3: Stop the Rooftop Solar Giveaway
Even though DC has almost no room to generate its own power, the DC Council has set aggressive requirements for local solar generation inside the city. This approach has added additional unnecessary fees to residential, commercial and industrial users throughout the city, while hindering overall clean energy adoption.

Electricity is like water. Fill a glass from three faucets and you can’t tell which sip came from where. RECs — renewable energy credits — are the label on the glass that tracks the source. Pepco or any other electricity provider in the district has to buy these RECs each year. If there are not enough, Pepco pays a fee. This fee establishes the maximum price for a REC: if a REC costs more than the fee, the provider just pays the fee instead. Most of DC’s renewable requirement is met through RECs for wind and solar projects throughout PJM. In 2024, that non-solar Tier I requirement cost about $87 million — covering 45% of the district’s electricity from renewable sources.[^6]

**DC ratepayers pay roughly the same for 3.65% of local solar as they do for 45% of all renewable energy across the grid. Read that again.**

To build a local solar industry, DC required that an ever-increasing share of RECs must come from DC rooftops — and set the price for these Solar Renewable Energy Credits (SRECs) very high. The demand side of this market just got a lot bigger: grandfathered electricity contracts that exempted a portion of DC load from SREC requirements have been phasing out, and that transition is now essentially complete. The result is a projected undersupply of 47,000 to 55,000 SRECs in 2026, regardless of how fast new solar gets built — which will push prices toward the $460 compliance fee ceiling.[^7]

**Here’s the kicker: companies must buy these credits even if they already supply 100% solar energy from outside DC — power that is up to ten times cheaper to certify. And Pepco doesn’t pay. You do. It’s buried in your generation cost, not listed separately.**

A typical DC solar system costs about $25,000. After a 30% federal tax credit, the owner paid about $17,000. DC SRECs traded between $365 and $435 in 2025, ending the year around $365. A typical 5 kW system generates about 6 SRECs per year — roughly $2,200 to $2,600 annually at current prices. Larger 8–10 kW systems can generate closer to $3,000 to $4,000. Either way, the system pays for itself well within a decade from SRECs alone, on top of electricity savings. After payback, that annual SREC income is pure gain. That’s a great deal for the homeowner. The question is who’s subsidizing it.

**This is a massive transfer of wealth to a few households.** No other jurisdiction has SRECs this high — because you don’t need them. Solar systems already pay for themselves over time through electricity savings alone. With a market-rate SREC, payback still happens — it just takes longer. And the cost borne by every other ratepayer would be much, much less.

In 2025, Pepco retired 128,175 SRECs to meet its compliance obligation — at a compliance fee rate of $460 per SREC. Across all DC electricity suppliers, the total SREC obligation was 407,748 SRECs. At average market prices of roughly $360 to $435 through the year, that’s approximately $147 million to $177 million in SREC costs across the district — costs passed directly to customers. **That number will go up: a projected undersupply in 2026 will push prices toward the $460 ceiling. Remember: Pepco doesn’t pay this. You do.** So when you hear the expression Pepco buys SRECs, remember:[^8]
##### You Buy SRECs
Only about 5% of DC households have solar. The other 95% are subsidizing them. The council could end the local solar requirement for any new renewable obligations immediately — stopping the SREC expense from growing. Existing SREC recipients would keep their credits only until their systems are paid off. That alone would drop costs significantly.

**The council wasn’t completely blind to the wealth transfer problem. They set up a program to fund free solar for low-income households** — financed by compliance fees paid when not enough SRECs are available. **But the program has only reached 15,000 of its 100,000-household goal. And there's a fatal design flaw: when enough local solar exists to meet the requirement, compliance fees disappear — and so does the funding.** You guessed it: no SREC shortage (and high SREC prices), no money for free solar.

**The Biden administration awarded DC $62.45 million through the Solar for All program to help.**[^9] **The Trump administration terminated the program in August 2025.** DC joined a multistate lawsuit challenging the termination. If DC loses, the free solar program — already massively backlogged — gets even less.
#### Fix #4: Make Rates Reflect Reality
This year the DC Council added a new Mayor’s Energy Surcharge to your bill — because the DC government had been raiding another energy fund to pay its own utility bills. That’s $10 million more on top of everything else. Not the biggest line item. But when you’re already drowning, every bucket counts.

Many of these programs serve a real purpose. Free solar for low-income households addresses real cost-of-living pressure. When the DC Council requires Pepco to collect fees, buy SRECs, or pay compliance charges, that’s a tax — just one nobody voted on and nobody labels honestly. Government programs should be funded through the income tax system, which is progressive: lower earners pay less. Utility rates are flat. Using your electricity bill to fund social programs hits low-income households hardest. Beyond that, rates themselves should reflect income. **The lower your income, the lower your rate.**
#### Summary
Reducing the cost of your electricity bill is a massive policy undertaking. It takes work on two fronts.
##### At The Federal Level:

1. Transition DC’s electricity system to a nonprofit public power authority.
2. Require data centers to bear the full cost of the power infrastructure they demand.
##### At the Local Level:
1. Replace the first class SREC mandate with an economy class renewable standard.
2. Fund clean energy programs through the income tax system — not utility bills.
3. Establish progressive electricity rates so lower-income households pay lower rates.

**Much of the electricity price increases is avoidable. Some of these fixes can stop the bleeding immediately. All of them together would lower your bill and still get DC to 100% clean energy.** The question isn’t whether we can afford to do this. It’s whether we have the will to stop using your electricity bill the wrong way.

## Naturally, No Taxation Until Representation
### A New Path to D.C. Statehood
As with many issues in Washington, new problems and solutions rarely exist. Knowledge doesn’t always pass across generations, and problems persist while “new” leaders appear to solve longstanding challenges with ideas that are simply revived from forgotten battles. I have learned in government to be humble enough to recognize this reality.

Yet even I am amazed at the extent to which D.C.’s status as a federal territory—a phrase we often omit because it sounds “less than”—has endured for centuries. As Ash and Musgrove state:

> “Washington was at once the capital of the world’s first republic in more than a millennium—and a city where slave labor was integral to economic life. It was a symbol of an aspiring democratic nation—and evidence of the racial limits of American democracy. It was a “citadel of liberty”—and a center of slavery and the slave trade…Another contradiction embedded into the fabric of the city harked back to the American Revolution. Self-government died early in the District. Not even a generation after Americans went to war to protest “taxation without representation,” Congress stripped Washingtonians of democracy’s basic unit of currency: the right to vote.”[^1]
#### The Limits of Sovereignty
It is not simply the lack of a vote or congressional representation. The District lacks the ability to govern itself as a sovereign political entity—a state. This is an old concern

> “The demise of political democracy—even the District’s highly restricted democracy—would haunt the city. Even the most elite Washingtonians remained supplicants who could petition, protest, and plead for congressional support and approval, but they could not hold their political patrons accountable. Within a few months of the city’s birth as the national capital, not even a full generation after the Revolutionary War, the disfranchisement of District residents was codified into federal law.”[^2]

Today, D.C. remains a territory gripped by the whims of a Congress that represents a much larger, disparate nation. We have become a laboratory for policy experiments by a majority that may hold social or economic philosophies entirely different from our own.
#### A Broken Justice System
When a D.C. resident is convicted of a local crime, they can be sent to a federal facility in West Virginia, Ohio, or beyond — hundreds of miles from their family, their community, and any support system that might help them return home whole. Beyond the lack of Senators or a Representative, we suffer from a lack of protection under the **Tenth Amendment**. Consider these “peculiarities” of our governance as a federal protectorate:
- **Federal Overreach:** National Guard troops have been deployed throughout the city ostensibly to combat crime, while Congress constantly seeks to undermine and oversee our local budget.
- **Judicial Backlogs:** While our local courts mirror state responsibilities, they are inherently federal. Judges are appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate, leading to vacancies and backlogs due to a dysfunctional nomination process.[^3]
    
- **Federal Incarceration:** The U.S. Attorney’s office prosecutes most local crimes, and federal prisons—often hundreds of miles away—incarcerate those convicted.[^4]
#### The Economic Injustice
No issue resonates more with the injustice of our status than taxation. Unlike other territories, D.C. residents pay federal income taxes. In fact, we contribute more per capita to the federal government than any other jurisdiction, yet we receive far less in benefits. **D.C. is a donor to the government with unequal say in how those funds are allocated**.[^5]

To make matters worse, Congress has prohibited us from collecting a **commuter tax**from those who work here but live in neighboring states. Burdened by their own high state taxes, representatives from Virginia and Maryland’s congressional delegates Maryland continue to block efforts to eliminate this ban. We are hamstrung in every aspect of taxation without a formal vote in Congress.
#### A New Mantra: No Taxes Until Representation
As we approach the 250th anniversary of our nation’s independence, D.C. remains mired in the same injustices that existed in 1780. We cannot allow another 250 years to pass before establishing our rights to state sovereignty.

Progress is possible—we were granted three electoral college votes through a constitutional amendment, and Congress once passed an amendment for full representation, though the states failed to ratify it. These successes show that a more just posture is achievable.

As Delegate, I will work to revive these efforts with a new focus. It is time to move past complaining about taxation without representation. Our new mantra must be: **No Taxes Until Representation**.
- **Legislative Action:** I will introduce legislation to exempt D.C. residents and businesses from paying federal income taxes.
- **Civil Disobedience:** Until Congress grants us full statehood and Tenth Amendment protection, D.C. businesses and residents should withhold federal income tax payments.

Let the Senators and Representatives explain to their own constituents why District taxpayers are no longer giving. Civil disobedience is always civil, but it is never obedient. We’ve been civil long enough; it is time to stop being obedient. Let this be the last tax day we pay into a system that refuses us a voice.

## Naturally, I'm Running for Congress
### Physics, Power, and the Momentum for D.C.
At a young age, I discovered the program _Cosmos_, sparking a fascination with science. I gravitated toward physics because it challenged me and explained wondrous natural phenomena. What other discipline could take the magic of a magnet and the explosiveness of a lightning bolt and distill them into a few elegant mathematical expressions? But, in graduate school, I realized the abstract world of theoretical particle physics was too removed from societal challenges. Before finishing my degree, I joined the graduate employee union at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, serving on the contract bargaining team to advocate for domestic partnership benefits. This was my first political action and it ignited a desire for service that I knew I had to pursue in Washington, D.C. From then on, I decided I’d use science to improve people’s lives.

Just weeks after defending my dissertation, I gave away my car and moved into a D.C. apartment I’d rented sight unseen. That was 27 years ago.

Becoming a “sharp-elbowed political player,” (as one magazine later described me) was never my goal. And when you stand up for people against powerful interests, you need grit. After I moved to D.C., my mother took every opportunity to tell friends, colleagues and members of Congress that I had once sworn I would never take a job that made me wear a suit. I did and I rolled up my sleeves and got to work.

A fellowship with the American Association for the Advancement of Science taught me to apply science to politics and policy in then-Congressman Ed Markey’s office. Working for Senate Majority Leader Harry Reid, I learned to fight and the art of coalition building. President Obama appointed me to head the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC)—a $1 billion agency with nearly 4,000 employees, **where I stood up to the most powerful industries** and helped the world respond to a major national nuclear accident, Fukushima. Since then, I have been working in the private sector, teaching and developing renewable energy projects.

But the re-election of Donald Trump and the feckless response from Congress to his corruption and brutality inspired my return to public service. With the retirement of Congresswoman Eleanor Holmes Norton, my calculus was simple: _Can I make a greater impact by running for office?_ I believe the answer is yes.
#### Defending the Federal Workforce
My chief of staff at the NRC kept a hat in his office that read “Young Bureaucrat.” It was a source of pride for him and an inspiration to me. As the head of a federal agency, I saw workers dedicated to the mission of serving the American people, prioritizing service over salary. While studies show federal workers often receive less compensation than their private-sector counterparts, they are driven by the vital task of helping all Americans.

Despite this, the current administration campaigned on insulting talking points regarding “trillions in waste”; claims I knew instinctively were false. **I was shocked by how quickly those falsehoods turned into policy.** By siccing Elon Musk and the “DOGE” team on federal workers to find fictitious savings, the administration has illegally dismantled agencies and abused hundreds of thousands of public servants.

The implications are dire. Decades of successful foreign policy have been erased. Tens of thousands of experienced scientists have fled the government, destroying future discoveries that could prevent diseases or drive economic prosperity. These reckless cuts dashed the optimism of the newly hired, who seek federal service as a noble pathway to a stable, secure middle class career path. Their dashed dreams will take decades to repair. I am running because I refuse to stand by while the “merit over patronage” system is erased. I will defend and rebuild the federal workforce with the respect and praise they deserve.
#### An Assault on Immigrants
The attacks on the federal workforce are paralleled by a brutal, illegal effort to detain undocumented people. This is personal for me. My father emigrated to the United States from Venezuela. He was born in Romania before World War II and spent his childhood wandering Europe as a refugee, moving in and out of displaced persons camps. His family eventually relocated to Venezuela in the 1950s.

Because of that journey, he spoke four languages, but at home, he spoke only English to my sister and me. He wanted to assimilate, yet he never lost the thick accent that marked his history. I was just a kid when my family went with him to a federal courthouse to become a citizen. His citizenship was a source of immense pride. When I witness the current assault on immigrants, I wonder what would have happened to my father under these brutal policies. The best way I can honor his legacy is to fight for a humane immigration policy that respects the diversity and hard work that immigrants bring to our society.
#### The Fight for D.C.
As the home of our nation’s capital, D.C. bears an outsized brunt of the administration’s attacks. Our residents live in fear of a lawless system of confinement that ignores the foundational protections of the U.S. Constitution.

Furthermore, the status of the District leaves our residents and businesses vulnerable to interference by officials elected elsewhere. **D.C. must become a state.** Statehood remains a mirage while National Guard troops roam our streets and Congress interferes with our local laws. Until we achieve statehood, we must address the injustice of federal taxation. No other American represented by a delegate in Congress pays federal income taxes. Why should we? D.C. is a net exporter of federal dollars, yet we lack a vote. Until we are granted the rights of statehood, D.C. residents should not be required to pay federal income taxes. Addressing this aspect of our lack of state sovereignty is another reason I am running.
#### A Data-Driven Future
**We must address the massive disparities in wealth and affordability.** This winter, D.C. electricity prices spiked far beyond the national average—a result of well-intentioned but misguided local programs and poorly thought-out federal policies. This unsustainable system harms our environment and saddles our most vulnerable residents with unmanageable bills and legacy pollution, while others prosper from overly generous, unnecessary subsidies.
