## Q1 — "What is this job? Name the things the delegate actually does."

(Answer order: Jaczko, Zalesne, Holbrook, Pinto.)

[Jaczko] Three buckets: (1) **constituent services** — the delegate is DC's only real House member; helps residents navigate e.g. Social Security; (2) **committee work** — has testified before Congress and staffed members; (3) **advocacy** — defend home rule and pursue statehood, "as I mentioned at the outset," via the **federal-income-tax exemption** ("$30B we send to the federal government that we don't get back"). "It's a position of soft power" — communication and coalition-building.
[Zalesne] Two big things beyond constituent service, and "luckily they rhyme: **autonomy and economy**." Autonomy = everything up to statehood (legislative autonomy, budget autonomy, a House floor vote, control of the National Guard, maybe a US Senate delegate). Economy = "we've learned the hard way we're a company town and the company hates us"; rethink DC's economy to be less federally dependent. "I'm the only one in this race who has held senior executive positions in the private sector." First-month commitment: **open a constituent-services office east of the river** (Norton's closed; the only one is near the White House).
[Holbrook] "I worked for Congresswoman Norton for the past eight years and I know how this position works" — Norton consistently ranked among the most effective lawmakers despite no final vote, because she knew process, bills, committees. Top priorities: statehood/equality on one side; fighting the "historic number of anti-home-rule attacks" on the other (>100 bills/resolutions this Congress, almost none enacted). Federal employees "under attack"; "I was a federal employee myself" and "fought against the illegal firings of our federal employees, fought for pay raises, fought for back pay after shutdown, fought for better retirement." "This isn't an entry-level position."
[Pinto] Three things: **legislation, constituent services, messaging.** Stresses the experience that matters "is not my experience as a health-and-aging fellow on the Hill working for Senator Blumenthal, or managing a frozen-yogurt shop, or working in senior-living communities — it is my experience serving you as councilmember for the last six years." "What the council does is work with communities to solve problems" — the best training for moving legislation, constituent services, and coalition-building on safety, schools, housing, and the economy.

## Q2 — "What is your strategy for building relationships on Capitol Hill to promote the district?"

[Zalesne] "The most important thing the delegate can do is have good relationships on Capitol Hill" — without formal power you win by bringing allies. "I have deep relationships across Capitol Hill for decades." DNC deputy national finance chair; co-chair Women for Kamala Harris. Three members have endorsed her (chosen to span right/left/center and local/regional/national). "I've raised money for these people, and that's how they reward you — better committee assignments." Relationships in the national business, nonprofit, and foundation communities too.
[Holbrook] Coalition-building, "and we've already done this." When he started with Norton, the statehood bill had been voted on once (1993, lost half the Democrats). In Norton's office they made it a national issue — got 100+ national organizations to endorse, record co-sponsors ("enough to pass the House on co-sponsors alone"). Worked with members on the House Oversight Committee ("the most important committee for DC — all anti-home-rule and pro-home-rule bills go through it"). "I know the members, the staff, the process."
[Pinto] As chair of the council's Committee on Judiciary & Public Safety after Congress overturned the Revised Criminal Code, she spent more time on the Hill; members (even Democrats) said "we'll overturn any public-safety law you pass." Her answer: **Secure DC** ("the largest piece of legislation in our city's history"), passed unanimously, "and Congress left us alone." Also beat back the Medicaid-match cut (70%→50%) and an attempt to bar DC from changing its criminal laws (pulled from the House floor). Endorsed by Sen. Alsobrooks ("one of our strongest regional allies") and Sen. Blumenthal. "These are not relationships we're starting from scratch."
[Jaczko] "Soft power" — true of any junior member. Approach: (1) **be proactive** — "if the first time you talk to a member or a union head is when you need something, that's never effective"; (2) **education** — explain how DC issues affect *their* constituents (e.g. a member from Massachusetts). "No secret sauce — it's a lot of work and a lot of communication." Wants to make the conversation concrete via federal taxation ("we pay taxes and have no vote").

## Q3 — Recent home-rule failures & successes: "What went wrong?" (the ~$1B budget freeze)

[Holbrook] "You're not going to win every fight in Congress" — use every loss to build the bigger argument. Example: after Jan. 6 / Lafayette Square, "we passed for the first time ever the DC National Guard Home Rule Act." On the budget attack: of >100 bills introduced, only a handful passed — "should be zero," but "not a bad track record." Warns the new delegate "is going to be the least senior member of Congress" and must know the legislative process.
[Zalesne] "If you're going to own the successes of Congresswoman Norton, you have to own the failures" — the ~$1B "stealing of our budget" was a huge failure; a GOP staffer inserted language barring DC from spending a billion of its own money "and we never got it back." Lesson: the seat "cannot just be reactive." Cites beating back the Medicaid 70%→50% cut as proactive work. "People are coming for us in a different way now."
[Pinto] Frames the freeze: Congress treated DC "like a federal agency" during DOGE's mass firings and froze DC's spending alongside federal agencies. "We got lumped in by mistake, arguably, but nobody was paying attention — not our delegate, not our DC Council." Argues for vigilance/foresight. (Also asserts DC "did get a bunch of that billion dollars back" — disputed below.)
[Jaczko] Two structural weaknesses: (1) no Tenth Amendment protection (not a state), so the delegate must fight on matters Congress shouldn't touch; (2) no two senators to filibuster, no House floor vote. Remedy: process knowledge, coalitions, leadership. Notes National Guard and federal agents are on DC streets "and we haven't been able to stop that." Mentions a current effort to curb DC's traffic cameras — "I'm sure a straw vote would say everyone hates them, but the principle is our ability to govern ourselves."

## Q4 — MPD crime-data manipulation scandal & judiciary oversight

[Pinto] "It is imperative that we have a local police department trusted by our community ... reporting accurate crime data" — for victims, for integrity, and because deployment decisions depend on it. Cites her fall oversight letter (all colleagues), ongoing oversight, and the chief placing **13 members (many senior) on administrative leave** facing likely termination. Touts MPD's daily public dashboard ("one of the most transparent in the nation"; FBI/federal agencies use the data). "I stand with our local police department ... but mistakes like this cannot be accepted."
[Jaczko] "Fascinating that in a congressional race we're talking about local crime statistics" — a quirk of no statehood; Oversight wouldn't probe NYC stats. Best step: **release the DC Inspector General report / the internal independent investigation** to learn how many stat changes were "inappropriate attempts to lower crime statistics" vs. normal investigative reclassification. "This is heavily politicized at the congressional level" — cites a misleading, one-sided report from Rep. [Zalesne] "There's one issue here: is DC embarrassed on the national stage or not?" To Pinto: "It is your responsibility as judiciary chair to make sure the crime facts are right. If you can't oversee a local agency, how will you oversee a federal agency? This job is not for someone who needs training wheels."
[Pinto, 30-sec reply] "Let's be careful not to use Republican talking points about our own local police department. We have the strongest police department in the country ... thousands doing hard work daily." The investigation found 13 members manipulating data, now in the administrative process; she's pushing the chief for systems "so this never happens again." "What we cannot do is adopt Donald Trump's talking points that everything is out of control."
[Holbrook] "As a DC resident I hear about this; as someone running for Congress I don't, because this is not what Congress should be doing." Argues the administration used crime stats to justify trying to federalize MPD. "What matters is what you'll do about it" — pass the **District of Columbia Police Home Rule Act** (which he's worked on for years) to make federalizing MPD explicitly illegal. "It was already illegal when the president tried; this makes it that much more obvious."

[Jaczko] "All these questions come back to statehood." Without the Tenth Amendment / senators, riders are hard to stop. Method: process/procedure knowledge, coalitions, and using "every tool" — but you must prioritize because "there will be more than you can fight."
[Zalesne] "These riders are performative, gratuitous, and way too easy to impose on us because we have no accountability over them." Two things: (1) **make it painful** — their constituents should know "their representatives are wasting time on us"; (2) build alliances. Then the recurring attack on council experience: "If you're on DC Council, the biggest coalition you've ever built is with six other DC Democrats — and more than half the time they fail at that ... that is not the best training for Congress to work with 435 members."
[Pinto] "I've heard you say this before, and it underscores your lack of understanding of how we operate in DC. The council is not a monolithic Democratic caucus."
[Holbrook] On the most recent appropriations bill there were "almost 20 riders" (e.g. trying to overturn DC's left-on-red laws); "we took it from 20 down to the two historic riders" (marijuana commercialization; the abortion-funding ban on local dollars). "I have built coalitions — 100+ national organizations on statehood ... women's groups who don't want abortion outlawed in DC. That's what real coalition-building is."
[Pinto] "Andy Harris, a Maryland congressman, is one of our biggest challenges on riders — his number-one priority should not be undermining DC. We need to organize around supporting his opponent." Calls for other Americans / regional stakeholders / oversight partners to have "a stake in our autonomy." "Some opponents give me a hard time for raising money around the country — I think that's important to build these coalitions."
[Zalesne, 30-sec] "To build a national coalition is a harder task than to bring six DC Democrats together."

## Q6 — Regional partners / commuter tax / federal-income-tax exemption

[Holbrook] "Being on the Hill, it's much easier to defend DC when DC is united" and every DC elected official has the delegate's back. On the commuter tax: "We should be able to tax people who work in DC ... but there are offsets" (e.g. the DC TAG dollars DC receives that states don't). Calls regional partners "some of our strongest allies on statehood" — Rep. Jamie Raskin (MD); cites a former Democratic leader who voted against statehood in 1993 then wrote a pro-statehood Washington Post op-ed. "They are among our strongest allies."
[Pinto] "No issue is more important than the safety of every resident ... getting illegal guns off our streets." Crime example: in Secure DC she changed pre-trial detention rules so judges *can* hold someone charged with a violent crime/homicide/rape pre-trial (with a written opinion) — "Republicans are now trying to reinstitute cash bail in the district," which she calls outrageous (measures ability to pay, not risk). "Our regional allies told me, 'Thank God you changed the pre-trial rules' — now they can vote against cash bail and say DC is responsible on crime." Understanding allies' "motivations and psychology" is key.
[Jaczko] "There's no unanimity among regional partners." Metro funding splits the region; the nonresident-income (commuter) tax "is regionally opposed — it's our regional partners, not the West Coast, blocking it." Returns to the federal-income-tax exemption: it "would give DC a competitive advantage" to attract business headquarters and "more resources to perhaps increase taxes locally" while still net-saving residents. "The underlying issue is fundamentally the lack of statehood."
[Zalesne] Proposes a **"Capital Caucus"** in Congress, driven by the DC delegate, with MD, VA, DE, PA — pitched to Jaime (Raskin?), Prince George's, and a Northern Virginia congressman, who were "enthusiastic." Rationale: regional economic growth (compete for national/global opportunities), plus shared healthcare (DC hospitals owned by MD/VA-headquartered companies) and environment (waterway pollution crosses borders). On the commuter tax "third rail," reframe it as a **"shared asset fund"** — make it reciprocal (DC residents who work in MD/VA pay in too) and earmark the funds for shared needs (environment, healthcare, Metro, roads/bridges). "Collect the tax and share it for shared expenses — win-win-win."

## Q7 — Federal land transfers & anti-displacement

[Pinto] "This is where it's important to have someone at the table representing our community." Example: vacant GSA buildings — opposes Trump moving federal agencies out, but DC's voice must shape what the land becomes "so those buildings don't just become hotels or luxury condos" but meet needs for senior housing, playgrounds, affordable housing; wants a master development plan connecting downtown to the Wharf. On the **RFK deal**, "proud to fight to secure benefits": 6,000 housing units (a third affordable), small/local/minority/women-owned contracting, fields kept open during construction. "The delegate is instrumental in transferring the land, but DC leaders must be at the table."
[Jaczko] "The question misunderstands the blurring of roles." There are 300+ federal reservations/parkland parcels in DC "that have no business being federal" — he'd work to transfer them all to DC, but **how the land is used "is a local decision"** for the council/mayor/community. Cautions against contingencies in federal transfers; "just transfer the land and let DC use local tools." On displacement broadly (rising values, beautification): reinvigorate special-credit / down-payment-assistance mortgage programs that the Trump administration restricted (limiting Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac purchases of such mortgages).
[Zalesne] "Land transfers are important to DC's economic growth ... but not the sum and substance of an economic-growth plan." The delegate's job is "autonomy and economy" — figure out how DC "comes out winners on the other side of Trump, DOGE, and the AI revolution." Two job sources: (1) a **homegrown tech scene** ("the kids closest to the problems will come up with the solutions"); (2) recruit large/medium tech companies — "when a big company chooses a second headquarters, it shouldn't choose Northern Virginia, it should choose us," with DC residents first in line for jobs/retraining.
[Holbrook] Agrees: transfer as much federal property to DC as possible, then "it's up to DC to decide." For untransferred land, work with the National Park Service / GSA to keep federal property maintained "the way DC deserves." Fight relocation of federal agencies out of DC — cites the first Trump administration moving part of USDA to Colorado and it returning months later. "This is where Congress is; the agencies need to be here."

## Q8 — Seniors / aging in place

[Jaczko] "Quality of life for people of all ages is fundamentally a local question." The federal piece: protect Social Security ("will it be viable in the future?") — "the most important thing we can do for seniors anywhere." Also the federal-income-tax exemption "would be a significant benefit for seniors." Argues the more the delegate is pulled into local issues, the less time for federal/national fights.
[Zalesne] "The one problem this country has *completely* solved is elder poverty — with Social Security." So with the right leadership "we can still solve social problems." Best thing a delegate can do: protect and shore up Social Security ("Republicans regularly want to undermine it"). Notes her husband is launching **pivoters.com**, a jobs board for people 55+ ("not all seniors are ready to retire ... elder workers are better than younger workers in many circumstances"), piloting in DC.
[Holbrook] "So much is local, but federal pieces matter — affordability is the biggest." Protect DC's federal funding and "make sure DC is treated as a state, not a territory," for senior programs, healthcare, etc. Protect Social Security and Medicare. Cites three years at the Corporation for National & Community Service (AmeriCorps Seniors) fighting GOP efforts to eliminate the program. "We need someone who knows how the executive branch and Congress work."
[Pinto] "We have options for very low-income seniors and for very high-income seniors, and we're leaving everyone else behind." Focus on aging in place: senior villages, rides to medical appointments, rising property taxes making it costly to stay in a long-owned home. Touts her council package **"Prosper DC"** (capping senior property taxes, apprenticeship programs) and her federal plan **"Breaking Ground DC"** (more affordable for first-time buyers and decades-long homeowners). Combat social isolation/loneliness; "understanding the distinction between what we can do locally and federally is fundamental."

## Q9 — Trump physically remaking DC (East Wing demolition, painting the reflecting pool, agencies "zeroed out") — how to combat it

[Zalesne] "At an aesthetic level, this is what tyranny looks like — you run roughshod over the laws and just do what you want, and he's doing that to our city." Combat it the same way: allies/partners in Congress ("they live here part-time, their staffs live here — it's their city too being destroyed"); take back the House and Senate. "It'll take a generation to reverse what he's done to some federal agencies ... that's the work of being American."
[Holbrook] Two things: (1) remove the federal agencies' authority (Commission of Fine Arts, the zoning/planning bodies) over DC land — or, failing that, remove the federal appointees so it's "controlled strictly by DC"; (2) he wants to serve on the **House Oversight Committee** and hold a hearing on this — "it's not just about the buildings, it's about the lawlessness," ignoring commissions and the rule of law.
[Pinto] Two things: (1) "make the case more effectively to Democrats *and* Republicans that a strong nation's capital is in their interest — most members in their hearts don't want DC to turn into 'Trump-land'"; (2) "find opportunities for success even in this administration" — cites a **$10 billion fund** DC may be able to secure, but "we have to be at the table" so the money goes "to our parks, seniors, children," not whatever Trump wants. "Being antagonistic to any proposal no matter who it comes from is not going to get us there."
[Jaczko] "Painting the reflecting pool blue is not the most significant issue. Launching an illegal war in Iran ... demolishing the East Wing ... dumping likely-contaminated dirt into East Potomac Park without warning" — these are the real violations. "The constitutional remedy for tyranny is **impeachment**, and when I get into Congress I will advocate strongly for impeaching Donald Trump." Criticizes Democratic leadership for tamping down impeachment talk before the midterms. "When the president won't follow the rule of law, impeachment is the ultimate remedy."
